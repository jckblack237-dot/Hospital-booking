What Vaguthu is

Queue software for clinics in the Maldives, and a free app for their patients.

Vaguthu (ވަގުތު, Dhivehi for time) is a Maldivian software product with two halves. One runs a clinic's queue at the reception desk. The other tells that clinic's patients when to leave home. Together they replace the paper register, the phone calls, and the two hours in a plastic chair. It is built for clinics from a single GP room on an island to a fifteen-doctor centre in Malé.

How one visit works

  1. Book. In the app, by phone, or by walking in. Every route lands in the same queue and gets a token like B-07.
  2. Get a window, not a time. The engine watches the doctor's real pace and tells the patient 19:05–19:25, updating as the evening unfolds.
  3. Leave when told. A push arrives when it is time to go, based on where the patient is waiting. The lobby stays empty.
  4. Be seen, pay, done. Reception calls the token, the doctor presses Start and End, and the bill and the Aasandha claim are built from the visit.

The problem

An ordinary evening at a Malé clinic today

A 16:00 appointment, booked three days ago. This is what happens to the patient who holds it.

  1. 15:45Arrives fifteen minutes early, to be safe. The lobby is already full.
  2. 16:00Appointment time. The doctor is not in yet. Nobody was told.
  3. 16:40The doctor arrives, forty minutes late. The queue starts.
  4. 17:20Three walk-ins go in ahead. One consultation runs 25 minutes over.
  5. 18:20Finally seen. Two hours and thirty-five minutes in a chair, for a ten-minute consultation.

Nobody lied to them. The queue simply lives on a paper register and in the receptionist's head, so the only way to know your position is to be physically present. For someone who took a ferry from Naifaru, that lobby is why they booked a hotel. The fix is not a better booking. It's a queue with a live, honest source of truth. With Vaguthu, the same patient stays home until 17:55, when their phone tells them to leave.

For clinics

The operational brain of the clinic

A dashboard the receptionist prefers to shouting across the room, and a screen the doctor uses without training. It works entirely on its own — no patient has to install anything for a clinic to get value.

The queue board

Walk-ins, phone bookings and app bookings in one ordered queue per doctor. Drag a card to move someone. Check in, call, and start with one tap. When a doctor is late, every waiting patient is told in seconds — by the system, not by the receptionist making eleven phone calls.

  • Colour means state only: green here, amber called, teal in the room, red moved back.
  • Delay penalties you configure, with an exemption for patients who came by boat.
  • Keyboard-first: W for a walk-in, ⌘K to find anyone.

The doctor's screen

One patient, two buttons. Start and End are what feed the wait-time engine — which is why they are the only things on the screen. "Need more time" pushes everyone back before the overrun happens.

  • Punctuality shown to the doctor first, always — as a scheduling input, never a leaderboard.
  • Never exposed to patients or partners. If it leaked, doctors would stop pressing the buttons.

Billing, claims, and what is stuck

Multi-payer by default — Aasandha, private insurers, corporate accounts and self-pay, with split billing as the normal case. Claims are built from the visit, rejections land in a worklist, and the clinic finally sees why claims come back.

Insights in words, not charts

"Dr Hassan's median consultation is 13 minutes but he is scheduled at 10. Moving to 12-minute slots would cut the average wait and cost two slots a session." The weekly digest tells the owner something they didn't know, every week.

How it works

The estimate is the product

A patient will leave the lobby only if they believe the number. They will believe it roughly twice before deciding it's noise. So the engine is built around being honest, not around being reassuring.

Consultations are skewed, and the engine knows it

Most take ten minutes; some take forty. Modelling the average is exactly what makes the back of the queue systematically late. We model the shape.

Running over makes the estimate grow, not go negative

A consultation already twenty minutes in is evidence of a complex case. Naive maths says "next patient at 18:40" at 18:52. Ours doesn't.

Prayer pauses are in the model

Five times a day the queue stops. An engine that treats a session as one continuous block is wrong five times a day. Pauses are first-class.

A window, never a point

Patients see 19:05–19:25, with a confidence. A single time is read as a promise, and every broken promise costs more than a range ever will.

"Leave now" leans early, on purpose

Ten minutes early costs a waiting room. Ten minutes late can cost the turn — and for a ferry passenger, the day. We buy the cheap error.

Every change carries a reason

"Running 17 min later — a consultation ahead of you ran long" reads as a clinic being a clinic. A number that changes on its own reads as incompetence.

Platform

An open API — including to other patient apps

It removes the strongest objection in the sale: "I don't want to lock my patients into one app." The clinic decides which partners may read its queue and how much of each session they may sell, and can revoke access in one click.

What is, and isn't, wired up

This is a working system with a real engine, real billing logic and a real API — and simulated counterparties. Aasandha, eFaas, BML, Ooredoo m-Faisaa and Viber are partnerships we do not yet have. Each sits behind an adapter with a working fallback, and every screen that touches one says so. Nothing here pretends otherwise.
